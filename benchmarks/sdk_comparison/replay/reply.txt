The harbor town wakes slowly on winter mornings. Fishing boats leave before dawn, and the first lights in the bakery come on soon after. By seven the market square fills with carts of bread, apples, cheese and dried fish, and the traders call out their prices over the noise of the gulls. A narrow road climbs from the square to the old lighthouse, which has not guided a ship in forty years but still stands white against the gray sky. Visitors walk up in the afternoon to look across the water toward the islands, where small farms grow potatoes and keep sheep on the steep fields. In the evening the wind usually drops, the boats come back with their catch, and the cafes along the harbor wall stay open late. People talk about the weather, the price of fuel and the school concert at the end of the month. Nothing in the town is hurried, and most of the families have lived here for generations. Those who leave for the cities often return in summer, when the days are long and the sea is calm enough for swimming. The town library keeps a small room of maps and photographs that show how the harbor has changed over two hundred years of storms and repairs.
